Recently, I had come across a video by *The Guardian* which showed a guy conducting street interviews with random people in Tel Aviv. The video showed scenes of beautiful Tel Aviv beaches, busy markets, and the vibrant nightlife, and seems to suggest a certain disconnect with the chaos and destruction in Gaza apparently only 60km away.

This got me thinking…

I have three thoughts I would like to explore. Firstly, on the thoughtlessness of humans and the banality of evil. Secondly, on the role of justice and responsibility in this conflict. And finally, what this means for me. Before I begin, I would preface that I am writing this as I think alongside it, without much revision, hence it may be more raw. Secondly also, I understand that this is a sensitive topic, and I am trying to tread carefully as I write this.

The first most pressing thought for me was the stark contrast between life in Tel Aviv, and the horror and destruction in Gaza. I think that was the intent of the video, showing the disconnect of the Israelis living in peace and luxury, while a war was happening next door. This immediately made me think of the phrase the banality of evil, coined by Hannah Arendt. For background, this phrase was used to describe how a high-ranking Nazi official, Adolf Eichmann, who had managed the logistics of sending many Jews to concentration camps, was just doing a very ordinary job in not very ordinary circumstances.

Similarly, it is very understandable that very ordinary Israeli people in Tel Aviv, would be living their very ordinary life, which may include going to the beach or clubbing, even while there is a not very ordinary war happening. It just goes to show that even with all the brilliance in technological advances, humans are generally thoughtless creatures. When I say thoughtless, I don’t mean irrational, what I am getting at is perhaps something along the lines of narrow-mindedness, or the failure to see things that are bigger than ourselves. In this case, it would be recognising the extent of suffering being created by the war.

A curious thought is that perhaps the thoughtlessness is an intentional choice, that it is uncomfortable to pursue these thoughts. In the video, there were times where the interviewer was rejected by avoidant phrases like “I don’t want to think about it”. In one scene, the interviewee was a solder who was at the war two days prior, and was just hanging out in Tel Aviv in a club to relax. This is very understandable, thinking about hard things is well, hard. It is only natural for the human brain to avoid such discomfort and pretend these things do not exist.

I think there is an underlying idea behind the video that stirs up deeper emotions. It is not just that Israelis are living a good life in Tel Aviv, but the sense that these Israeli could do something to lessen the suffering in Gaza. This comes to the second point on justice and responsibility. Who is responsible for such suffering in the region? Who created the chaos?

It is very difficult to answer this question, but the broad answer is that both sides have contributed to the situation now. The video I watched seems to portray a bit of a negative light on Israel towards situation in Gaza, and I have identified a possible line of reasoning why. The line of reasoning follows below:

1. There is massive suffering in Gaza, with destruction and unethical killings, caused by the IDF.
2. The average Israeli in Tel Aviv appears to be living in privilege and nonchalance towards the suffering in Gaza.
3. The average Israeli in Tel Aviv is proximal enough to help to reduce the suffering in Gaza.
4. Reducing suffering is a morally responsible thing to do.
5. If 1, 3 and 4, then the morally responsible thing for the average Israeli in Tel Aviv to do is to try to reduce the suffering in Gaza.
6. Hence, since the average Israeli in Tel Aviv is not doing that (as they appear to be living in privilege and nonchalance), there is moral blame for the average Israeli towards the suffering in Gaza.

This is a very simplified argument, that is based on many assumptions. But I hope it is still reasonable enough to capture why there is some discomfort in seeing the very ordinary life of the Israeli when compared against the destruction in Gaza. It is almost an “Israel should be facing the same suffering” kind of sentiment.

When taking a step back, I am hesitant to blame the average Israeli. After all, they are just ordinary people living their ordinary lives. Is living out an ordinary life evil? To a citizen of Nazi Germany it may be, but in this case it is not so clear. It is, after all, the leaders that are directing the conflict. What is clear, is that we as humans can never escape thoughtlessness and selfishness. We will always do evil in our own banality, whether small or big, and whether it is even valuable to assign moral blame is difficult to ascertain.

I have now reached the third and final part which I was dreading, self-introspection. I think as a Singaporean living 8000km away from Gaza, this topic definitely has not crossed my mind very often. I too, am a very ordinary Singaporean living a very ordinary life, and I have been very thoughtless towards the whole Israel and Gaza conflict, preferring not to read about it so as to avoid any confrontation. Should I have thought about it more? Should I have tried to do things? Is my lack of proximity an excuse for inaction in my ordinary life? Peter Singer would probably have said it was an excuse, and that I should have been doing more to help.

Well, I don’t know the answer to whether I should care more, but here are some things I do know. First, life has too many problems that needs caring about. In the space of all possible problems, there are just too many problems. I physically am incapable of contributing to every problem I see. I even have my own problems to solve (surprise). Second, the media is sensationalised and globalised. If I were living like 100 years ago, I probably wouldn’t even know of such news. Now I can stream HD live footage of war and suffering straight to the comfort of my bedroom, all thanks to technology. I am not sure this is a good thing to be consuming for the mind, and I am not sure if I want to be consuming such things. Third, deep knowledge and strong views does not equate to care. I can be an expert on the history of Israel and Palestine, or be an activist towards better humanitarian rights for the suffering people. But these are not markers of care and concern. It is one thing to say a certain nation is wrong, it is another to see suffering children on my phone screen, and it is yet another thing to actually serve and help others. I guess what I am getting at is it is very easy to see people suffering and saying “this is wrong”, but most of us won’t get our hands dirty, we would just live our normal ordinary lives doing ordinary things.

All this just makes me think so much more of how much we need the gospel. Like truly life does not make sense without Jesus, and relationship with the Father. Is my Singaporean life really just going to work 44 hours a week, finding 2 weeks each year for holidays, saving enough money to afford housing, car, a wedding, children’s education, etc. Is life just getting amused with world leaders not getting along, watching wars in high-definition and feeling pity, and doomscrolling for funny videos everyday. There must be more to life than this.

I think it is when I take a step back from “ordinary life” that it is more clear what is truly ordinary life. It is not about pursuing hobbies or feeling good all the time or building social connections, all of which are good things which make life enjoyable. Instead ordinary life is about living life with God’s presence. It is the very life that Jesus came to bring, which is life to the full!

I will still return to my ordinary ways tomorrow, and the day after, and for days to come. But I guess I will continue to walk on this journey of life and see where it leads, and hopefully grow along the way. :)

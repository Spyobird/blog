I’m back with another set of opinions for the book/audiobooks I consumed over Sep and Oct. A little long overdue as I have been getting a bit slow/lazy with reading. Hope you enjoy!

# *The Ruthless Elimination of Hurry* by John Mark Comer & Kris Koscheski

This book is a Christian book that encourages a slower & simpler way of living, especially in a chaotic modern society. The book encourages its readers to “ruthlessly eliminate hurry” from their life, because “hurry is the great enemy of the spiritual life.” Instead, to live deliberately as a Christian involves slowing down and following after the lifestyle of Jesus.

The book made three powerful points that stuck with me. The first is that many Christians, myself included, want the restful life of Jesus without pursing the restful lifestyle of Jesus. We think Christ is important, but so are things like work, leisure, friendships, family, etc. We end up being too distracted with good things that we don’t do the things that matter. The next, is that desire cannot be the driver of life. Our infinite desires, coupled with our finite human self, leads to restlessness. Our soul is restless till it finds rest in God. The final point is that the sabbath is blessed because it is rest. We must learn to work from rest and not for rest. This book has made me notice how much I constantly pursue busyness, and how uncomfortable I feel not being busy. I am definitely still learning to slow down and learn to live life to the fullest.

With Singapore being quite a hectic country to live in, I think this book is applicable to many Christians here who need a new perspective of work and rest. This book is easy to follow, but applying what the book prescribes can be difficult without deliberate action and self-reflection.

# *The Courage to Be Disliked* by Fumitake Koga & Ichiro Kishimi

This book’s main idea is that every individual has the power to shape their life into what they want, regardless of their past experiences. This is encouraging, because we can be free from our past and other people in deciding what brings us happiness and belonging. The book brings insights from Adlerian psychology, and it is written in a fun dialogue manner.

One of the ideas in the book is that humans tend to wish that they could be more like someone else. This is because they feel like they are missing something, and are under a false assumption that they would be happier if they had this. This is just indication that we have not learnt to accept ourselves, and the book goes so far as to say that in this case we are choosing to remain stuck. This stood out to me, because I do notice myself subconsciously comparing myself with others and wishing I was smarter, more extroverted, better at music, etc. Another small but interesting thought is that giving praise to others is manipulative because the one praising feels implicitly greater in ability to the one being praised. Instead, gratitude is much better than praise.

This book is on the more intellectual side of the pop psychology spectrum, and it makes a stance for viewing the world with an Adlerian psychology lens. This makes it a bit more challenging to derive practical insights compared to traditional self-help books. I think this book is targeted at people who feel inferior to others or stuck in life (which if we are honest, is probably all of us at some point), helping us to be free of these. With all that said, the book is rich with depth and insight, far deeper than what I can unpack here. I think it can be a rewarding book for those that are willing to delve into its crevices, but personally I have not done so.

# *The Irish Goodbye* by Heather Aimee O’Neill

One of the few fiction books I have read. I only found this book on audible because it was first, a bestseller, second, on a deal, and third, not very long. This book tells a story of a family of sisters, as they navigate adult relationships, both familial and romantic, and how they deal with a past traumatic event.

What I liked about this book was that it was quite an easy read (listen). The plot was also relatable, as it was written alternating between the perspectives of the sisters, and the whole setting of the book was very ordinary. This ordinary setting really contrasts with the complexity of some of the conflicts in the book, and it made me reflect on the complexities of the world we live in, especially in familial relationships. One example of this is the dysfunction that each character develops, such as being divorced, aborting an unwanted pregnancy, and coming out of the closet, to name a few. And how these develop into each character’s personality and affect how they relate to others. Much of the book is similarly bringing out different aspects of humanness.

I do think this is quite a good book, and I would recommend it for people who want to read a little bit of fiction, but don’t want to get started with a whole series. It is a self-contained story in itself, and it is overall quite an easy read, but it also does pull up some deeper themes to think about.

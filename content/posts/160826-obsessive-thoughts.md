I am not a horror person, I do not do well with scary shows. But some friends convinced me that Obsession was not like the other horror movies because it "was not supernatural" and "made you think deeply" . They suggested I should give it a watch and let them know what I had thought about it.

And so I did. And it was terrifying. And these are my thoughts.

Before I continue, I must first admit I absolutely chickened out at some parts of the show. I was cowering behind pillows, trying to distract myself with my phone, and watching with my peripheral vision, because I anticipated some moments were quite scary. In hindsight, it was pretty funny how scared I had felt.

Obsession is a show brilliant in using tension and unease to carry a sense of horror to the audience. The central theme revolves around the lack of autonomy and how that can be quite scary. Through these musings I will first share my opinions of what the film did well to make viewers like me so scared, which comes from an effective manipulation of tension within the show. Then I hope to share why this film does in fact "make you think deeply", touching on some of the underlying human themes that are hinted at.

SPOILERS AHEAD!!!

A big part of what made Obsession so frightening, is how the film manipulates tension by subverting expectations. The film did this effectively in three ways. Firstly, it built tension by portraying Nikki as a looming threat. Secondly, the film used Nikki's physical movements to create moments of unease. And finally, it used "real Nikki" moments to create unexpected scares.

The foundation of this film's horror rides mostly on Nikki's ever looming presence. The film does a good job using a series of expertly planned scenes to build tension and remove any sense of safety. Early on in the film, we get shown many scenes with Nikki just there in the background. This might be just her standing off in the back staring at Bear, or seeing her silhouette through a window. This gives the viewers the sense that Nikki's presence is always felt, and that nowhere is really safe, like a living nightmare. These scenes help to build tension without ever dispelling it, keeping the viewer perpetually on edge.

The film quickly ramps up in tension and unrest as it starts showing Nikki's obsessive nature, while Bear starts distancing himself from her. The viewer starts to see Nikki push the boundaries in her obsessiveness, and Bear push the boundaries of what he can get away with despite Nikki's current state. There were some memorable scenes which captured this unsettling feeling. First, was when Nikki taped up the front door. When I watched that a huge chill shot up my spine, because of the juxtaposition of how normal the prior conversation between Bear and Nikki seemed to be, with the reveal of how strong Nikki's devotion actually was. Second, was a scene shortly after, when Bear was interacting with Sarah at work. Somehow there was a looming sense of dread that Bear was making some poor decisions, and that something bad was about to happen. The best part is that nothing really happens in these interactions, but they serve to continue building tension in the film.

On top of the tension building, the film taps on unnatural physical movements to create a sense of eeriness and unfamiliarity. There were a few scenes with Nikki moving unnaturally. The memorable ones are the coat, the flower pot, and the doorway, if you know you know. These scenes are effective in bringing out the alienness of "stalker Nikki", and it contrasts well with the Nikki in the other interactions, where she's mostly just putting on an overly-perfect smile. In the entire film, the unnatural movement was the most traditional horror element. And that is also what makes these scenes leave a lasting impression. They serve to remind the viewer to not get too comfortable with Nikki.

Finally, the film subverts expectations of safety through "real Nikki" moments. These are the brief moments where we see the real Nikki momentarily break out from her trapped state. Typically, these scenes come as a jumpscare, giving a good rush of adrenaline to the viewer. The notable example is the house party scene. Nikki is her usual weird self and gets a kiss from Bear and everything seems to be calming down, before "real Nikki" emerges cued with some loud sound effects to jolt the viewer from getting too comfortable. Up until this point, most of the dread is felt when the viewer does not see Nikki on the screen. Ironically, this leads to a sense of safety when Nikki is visible on screen. These "real Nikki" moments function to disrupt that predictability. Even with a full view of Nikki, one can never predict what is going to happen next.

There are bound to be many more techniques that the film uses to build up the atmosphere. These were most prominent in engineering the tension within the film. The result is a subversion of horror expectations, creating tension that often leads nowhere, and then giving the scare when there is a sense of familiarity. Together, these make Obsession a film that succeeds at keeping the viewer on edge from start to end.

More than the horror, there is a deep philosophical element to the film. This comes from the hyperbolic expression of relatable human themes. Specifically, the overarching theme of the loss of autonomy, and sub-themes of selfishness, desire, and delusion. Because these themes are presented in a relatable way, but pushed to the extreme, it gives the viewer the empathic sense that "this could be me".

Needless to say, the main theme through the show is the horrors of the loss of autonomy. This is fully embodied in Nikki's obsession over Bear. Ironically, Bear also gets trapped by his own wishes and cannot escape the situation without some drastic measures (which he is unwilling to do). This theme is explored from these two angles, from Nikki who has lost all control of herself, to Bear who has his freedom limited by Nikki.

Nikki has lost all autonomy to make good decisions, and the film expresses that this loss is deeply disturbing. First, it is easy to forget that this was non-consensual, because it was all the effect of the "one wish willow". Bear who was oblivious at the start, definitely at some point recognises that it was the wish that was causing Nikki to behave in that way. Yet he continued to stay in the relationship, making his actions an intentional violation of consent. Why this does not appear so evident is because secondly, the expression of non-consent is subverted. In the film, Nikki is the one making all the romantic moves on Bear, making it seem like Bear is the victim. This is what makes it so disturbing, it is actually Bear that is the offender, since he used the "one wish willow" from the start, but the expression of offense appears from Nikki. This gets a bit philosophical because it touches on the depths of the nature of autonomy.

As a result of Bear's wish, Nikki crosses many personal boundaries with him. This manifests in the show as typical stalker behaviour, such as Nikki watching Bear as he sleeps, or her looking through the CCTV cameras. The unsettling bit is how losing one's boundaries makes it feel like one also loses their safety. Eventually this manifests in Bear wanting to escape from the situation but finding no good way to do so besides ending his life or Nikki's, both of which he does not want to do. This is relatable because it happens in real life, we hear stories of stalkers on the news, or friends unknowingly crossing boundaries. And this show does the job of painting that feeling of being trapped and vulnerable.

Besides the lack of autonomy, the film also shows Bear's desire turning into selfishness and delusion. The film's premise is Bear desiring Nikki but not having the courage to ask her out. As a result, he turns to the cheap way out which is the "one wish willow". After the wish, his delusion continues to be so strong in that even though he knows something is wrong with Nikki, he continues to pursue the relationship. This is Bear's hamartia, that his selfishness and delusion over Nikki causes him to prioritise his wants, disregarding any concern for Nikki or his other friends. In other words, Bear chooses his own ego over other's agency.

The film makes it clear that Bear is both cowardly and selfish. In the film, when calling the "one wish willow" hotline, he asks about how to reverse half the effects of the wish. In essence, he still wants Nikki for himself, minus all the bad consequences of it. Later on, when the "real Nikki" asks him to put an end to her life, Bear replies whether it really was so bad to be with him. At this point, Bear is fully aware the wish is making Nikki who she is, and furthermore that the "real Nikki" would have rejected him, hence he continues to choose the "obsessed Nikki" for himself. This hits home because we can see ourselves in Bear's shoes, really desiring something for our own ego. We need not make as extreme choices as Bear, but it is unsettling that we could picture ourselves making similar judgements as him.

Obsession is a film rich with themes to explore. The hollowness of desire, the illusion of niceness, to name a few. The film effectively touched on the loss of agency and its horrors, and on the nature of human desire, selfishness and delusion, and how that can be relatable yet troubling. These themes are pushed to the extreme in the film, simmering in the minds of viewers for time to come. Overall I thought it was a brilliant film that focuses on the extremes of blind love and human desire. It builds up a case that human nature thrives on agency and free choice, and how losing that equates to losing what it means to be human.

Will I ever rewatch the film? I'm good.
